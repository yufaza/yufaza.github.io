Project case study

Kinspire

An employee improvement platform that connects Kaizen ideas, implementation evidence, multi-stage review, evaluation, and recognition in one traceable workflow.

01

Turn employee ideas into visible improvements

Kinspire supports the company’s continuous-improvement process, giving employees a structured way to propose a Kaizen idea, obtain review, record implementation, and see the evaluated result. It connects individual participation with the people responsible for review, scoring, and recognition.

The application keeps the original problem, improvement objective, implementation evidence, evaluation, and reward progress together. Its pipeline views and leaderboards make participation and follow-through visible beyond the individual submission.

The thinking behind Kinspire

Small improvements deserve a path to happen

A useful improvement does not have to begin as a major project. An employee might notice an unclear label, repeated data entry, an awkward handover, or a tool that takes too long to find. These are illustrative examples of everyday friction: small enough to overlook individually, but worth addressing when they recur.

Kaizen means continuous improvement. It involves the people doing the work in finding better ways to perform it, testing changes, and learning from the result. Its everyday practice makes progress through repeated, manageable changes that can build on one another over time. Larger initiatives still have a place; the point is to make improvement a regular habit as well.

Why this matters for an employee platform

Kinspire gives that habit an operational home. Employees can describe a local problem and propose an achievable change without first turning it into a large project proposal. Review gives the idea attention, implementation evidence makes the result visible, and recognition acknowledges the contribution.

The important outcome is the change in the work itself. A high submission count or leaderboard position can show participation, but does not establish that a process became safer, clearer, quicker, or more reliable. The original problem and the observed result give recognition its meaning.

The learning loop: Plan → Do → Check → Act

PDCA turns improvement into a learning cycle. Plan identifies the problem and intended change; Do tries it; Check compares the result with the expectation; Act retains what works or adjusts the approach before another cycle. A successful change should become part of the normal way of working so its benefit can last.

  • Make the opportunity concrete

    Kinspire records the current condition, objective, and proposed improvement. That gives reviewers a clear reason for the change.

  • Make the implementation visible

    The result submission and supporting photos connect the idea with what was actually done.

  • Make the learning worth following up

    Evaluation and recognition give the implementation a reviewable outcome. Carrying a successful practice into daily work is the wider organizational follow-through; a reward alone does not complete that learning.

That is why Kinspire matters: it accommodates the small improvements employees can make repeatedly, giving them a route from observation to action and recognition. Over time, those contributions can become a sustained improvement practice across the organization.

Kaizen and PDCA background: Lean Enterprise Institute — Kaizen. The examples above illustrate the philosophy; they are not claims of measured Kinspire results.

02

Keep an improvement moving beyond submission

Collecting ideas is only the beginning. A proposal needs an appropriate reviewer, an implementation needs evidence, and recognition needs a result that can be evaluated consistently. Different people take responsibility at each stage, so the system must show what has happened and who should act next.

Kinspire models those handovers explicitly. Approval to implement, submission of the actual result, completion of scoring, and completion of the reward are separate states rather than a single generic approval flag.

03

Key features

  • Structured employee submissions

    Employees describe the current condition, improvement objective, work area, improvement type, estimated cost, and planned implementation dates. Employee context and team membership support a clear record of who proposed the improvement.

  • Section Head and CI/PDCA review

    A Section Head reviews the proposal before the CI/PDCA team performs the next gate. Approval and rejection decisions are recorded as workflow transitions, with a rejection reason where relevant.

  • Implementation evidence

    After approval, the proposer submits the implementation result with supporting photos. This separates what was proposed from what was actually carried out and gives evaluators a basis for their review.

  • Assigned evaluators and final assessment

    CI/PDCA assigns evaluators individually or in bulk. Structured criteria support score breakdowns, with final CI/PDCA assessment and a Factory Manager evaluation path where applicable.

  • Reward follow-through

    The evaluated result records the final score and reward amount. A pending-reward stage remains separate from the rewarded state, making recognition a tracked step after assessment.

  • Pipeline analytics and leaderboards

    Overview, pipeline, process, and evaluation views support follow-up across the improvement lifecycle. Employee and department leaderboards show participation, while pipeline exports support further review.

  • Role-based actions and notifications

    Proposers, Section Heads, CI/PDCA, evaluators, and Factory Managers have distinct permitted actions. Email notifications support submission and status-change follow-up.

04

A clear path from proposal to recognition

The workflow is designed around handovers: the proposer explains the opportunity, reviewers decide readiness, the team implements, and evaluators assess the evidence. Each step has a different responsibility.

  1. Explain the improvement opportunity

    The employee records the problem, objective, proposed improvement, estimates, and team details, then submits the idea for review.

  2. Complete the review gates

    The Section Head and CI/PDCA team review the proposal through their respective queues. The approved path moves toward implementation; rejected decisions retain their reason.

  3. Submit what was implemented

    The proposer records the result and supporting photo evidence. CI/PDCA coordinates evaluator assignment for the submitted implementation.

  4. Evaluate and complete recognition

    Assigned reviewers score the result using the applicable criteria and evaluation path. The system records the final evaluation, then tracks the reward through completion.

05

My contribution

I developed the employee-facing submission workflow and the backend services that connect review, implementation, evaluation, and reward tracking. The work turns the improvement process into explicit states and role-specific actions rather than a collection of disconnected forms.

I also worked on evaluation and reporting: connecting criteria and reviewer assignments with score breakdowns, final assessment, pipeline visibility, and leaderboards. The implementation keeps the evaluated result and recognition progress connected without treating them as the same event.

A key engineering decision is to validate the current workflow state when performing a transition. Recording the state change and its history together helps maintain a traceable improvement record as different roles act on it.

06

Workflow state and evidence behind every score

Kinspire uses an Angular and TypeScript frontend with Express services running on Bun. PostgreSQL and Prisma support the improvement records, workflow history, evaluator assignments, scoring, and application permissions. Keycloak integration provides company sign-in, and email services support workflow notifications.

The service layer validates which role can perform an action and which workflow state allows it. Transactional updates connect important state transitions with their history, while the scoring layer exposes structured evaluation results for the application and analytics.

The same improvement record links proposal, implementation, and evaluation context. Analytics can then describe where work sits in the process, rather than presenting only a final total detached from its source.

07

Outcomes & engineering lessons

Kinspire provides a common workflow for employee participation, management review, and recognition. Employees can follow their idea beyond submission, while the review teams can inspect the pipeline, evaluation progress, and reward status.

The central lesson is that continuous improvement needs both encouragement and accountability. A leaderboard makes participation visible, but the implementation evidence, review history, and scoring breakdown explain what the recognition represents.
